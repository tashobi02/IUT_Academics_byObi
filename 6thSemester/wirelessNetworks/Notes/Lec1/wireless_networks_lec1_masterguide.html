Lecture 1 — Foundation

What is Wireless Networking?

Wireless networking is the transmission of data between devices using electromagnetic radio waves instead of physical cables. The fundamental objective is to move a message from point A to point B with the required quality, at minimum cost.

Quality measures
BER · Delay · Data Rate
Resource costs
Power · Bandwidth · Complexity

Why does wireless matter?

Three key motivations: eliminates physical cabling in difficult areas · enables user mobility · lower installation cost in many environments

The three course questions

How does a wireless network work?

Architecture, protocols, signal propagation

What are the limitations?

Attenuation, interference, fading, mobility, security

How do we address them?

MIMO, QoS, WPA3, adaptive modulation, mesh networks

Wireless is a broadcast medium

Unlike wired, all wireless devices share the same air. Every transmission can be heard by everyone nearby — this is both a feature (no cabling) and a source of most wireless problems (interference, security, hidden terminals).

Wired vs Wireless

Understanding the key differences helps you understand why wireless is harder to engineer than wired.

Most important insight

In wired networks, each cable is a separate, dedicated channel. In wireless, everyone shares one medium — the air. This single fact explains most wireless challenges.

Why CSMA/CD fails in wireless

In Ethernet (wired), a device listens while transmitting and can detect if its signal collided. In wireless, the transmitting node's own signal drowns out any other signal it might try to detect — so collision detection is impossible. Instead, Wi-Fi uses CSMA/CA — it avoids collisions by waiting and using random backoff timers before transmitting.

Key insight: one-size-fits-all protocol design does NOT work for wireless — voice tolerates high BER but needs low delay; email tolerates long delay but needs very low BER.

Barrier 1

Signal Attenuation

Attenuation means the signal gets weaker as it travels. In wireless, this happens in two ways: signals disperse as they travel farther, and they lose energy as they pass through matter.

Intuition: Think of a flashlight. The farther you are, the dimmer and more spread-out the light. Walls block even more. Radio waves behave the same way.

Three propagation ranges

Transmission range

Signal is strong enough for reliable communication. Low error rate.

Detection range

Signal can be sensed but not decoded. No useful communication possible.

Interference range

Signal cannot even be detected — only adds noise to other transmissions.

Fading types

TypeCauseTimescale
Path lossFree-space spreading over distanceSlow / distance
Slow fading (long-term)Large obstacles, shadowingSeconds to minutes
Fast fading (short-term)Multipath interference, small movementMilliseconds

Why higher frequencies attenuate more

Higher frequency signals are absorbed more by walls, rain, and air molecules. This is why 5 GHz Wi-Fi is faster but has shorter range than 2.4 GHz — it attenuates faster through obstacles.

Impact on BER

Lower received signal → lower SNR → harder to distinguish 1s from 0s → higher bit error rate. The receiver needs the signal to be above a minimum threshold called receiver sensitivity.

Solution strategies

MIMO antennasMesh networksRepeaters/relaysIncrease TX powerAdaptive modulation

Barrier 2

Noise & Interference

Even if your signal reaches the receiver, if competing signals or random noise corrupt it, the receiver cannot correctly decode your bits.

Four categories of noise

Caused by the random motion of electrons in any conductor. Present in ALL electronic devices. Increases with temperature and affects all frequencies uniformly. You cannot eliminate it, only manage it.

Real world: This is why electronics run cooler in refrigerated server rooms — less thermal noise.

Sudden short-duration high-energy spikes. Caused by lightning, switching equipment, or power line disturbances. Particularly harmful to digital communication — can corrupt multiple bits at once.

Real world: Your Wi-Fi drops right when a microwave turns on nearby.

Occurs when signals at different frequencies mix in a nonlinear device, producing additional unwanted signals at sum/difference frequencies. Affects amplifiers and mixers.

Unwanted signal leakage between adjacent cables or channels. More common in wired systems but can occur in wireless due to adjacent channel interference.

ISM Band — the crowded spectrum

ISM = Industrial, Scientific, Medical band

License-free spectrum — anyone can transmit here with no regulatory approval (just power limits). This makes it crowded and interference-prone.

FrequencyUsers
2.4 GHzWi-Fi (802.11b/g/n), Bluetooth, microwave ovens, baby monitors
5.8 GHzWi-Fi (802.11a/n/ac), radar, ISM devices
915 MHzIoT, Zigbee, LPWAN

SINR formula

SINR = S / (N + I)

S = Signal power  ·  N = Noise power  ·  I = Interference power

Large SINR → easier to extract signal from noise → lower BER

Solution strategies

Directional antennasAdaptive frequency selectionCognitive radioSmart AP placementQoS policies

Barrier 3

Multipath Fading

Radio waves don't travel in a straight line only. They bounce off buildings, cars, trees, and the ground, taking many different paths to reach the receiver — each with a different delay. When these copies add up at the receiver, they can reinforce or cancel each other.

Intuition: Imagine dropping a stone in a pond. Ripples reflect off the walls and overlap. Some waves add up (constructive), others cancel (destructive). Your Wi-Fi signal does exactly this in a room full of walls.

Three propagation mechanisms

Reflection (λ ≪ D)

Signal bounces off large surfaces (walls, floors). Wavelength much smaller than obstacle. Most common effect.

Diffraction (λ ≈ D)

Signal bends around edges of obstacles. Wavelength similar to obstacle size. Enables coverage around corners.

Scattering (λ ≫ D)

Signal scatters off small objects (rain drops, foliage). Wavelength larger than obstacle.

Fast vs slow fading

TypeCauseSpeed of changeEffect
Fast (short-term) fadingConstructive/destructive interference between multipathsMilliseconds (small movement)Rapid power fluctuations
Slow (long-term) fadingMoving behind a large obstacleSeconds/minutesGradual average power change
Path lossDistance spreadingVery slowMonotonic power decrease

Impact on BER

Multipath causes inter-symbol interference (ISI) — a copy of an earlier symbol arrives while the next symbol is being received. This blurs the signal, increasing errors. At worst, perfect cancellation can completely null the signal even at close range.

Real-world example

Your phone has perfect signal outside a building. You step inside and signal drops sharply — you've moved from constructive to destructive multipath interference. Walking 30cm in any direction may improve it again.

Barrier 4

Doppler Shift (Mobility)

When either the transmitter or receiver is moving, the received frequency differs from the transmitted frequency. This is the same effect as a police siren sounding higher-pitched as it approaches you and lower as it moves away.

Intuition: If you're running toward a speaker, you hit more sound waves per second than if you were standing still. More waves per second = higher perceived frequency. Radio works the same way.

How Doppler causes high BER

Carrier frequency offset

Received frequency ≠ expected frequency → receiver loses lock on signal

Synchronization loss

Timing references drift → receiver decodes bits at wrong moments

Increased channel variation rate

Channel changes faster than the receiver can track → unpredictable errors

Doppler effect chain

Movement → frequency shift → carrier offset → sync errors → symbol misinterpretation → ↑ BER

Real-world example

On a fast train with LTE, the base station at 1800 MHz may experience ~100 Hz Doppler shift. For fast-moving vehicles or aircraft, this can reach several kHz — enough to cause significant decoding errors without correction.

Handover problem

As a mobile user moves between access points, the network must switch the connection — this is called a handover. Each handover can cause latency spikes or brief packet loss, especially problematic for real-time voice calls.

Solution strategies

Doppler compensation algorithmsFast handoff techniquesMobile IP supportPilot-based channel tracking

Barrier 5

Hidden Terminal Problem

This is one of the most important wireless-specific problems. It arises because nodes can only detect signals from their neighbors — not from everyone.

Classic scenario: A–B–C

A and C are both in range of B, but A and C are out of range of each other. A thinks it's safe to transmit to B (no one is sending). C also thinks it's safe. Both transmit simultaneously — their signals collide at B, corrupting the data. Neither A nor C can detect this collision because neither can hear the other.

Why this doesn't happen in wired networks

In Ethernet, all devices share a single wire. When any device transmits, every other device detects the signal with sufficient strength. CSMA/CD works perfectly — collisions are detected and corrected. In wireless, signal strength drops with distance, so A and C genuinely cannot hear each other.

PropertyWiredWireless
Can all nodes hear each other?YesNot always
Hidden terminal problem?NoYes
Collisions detectable at sender?Yes (CSMA/CD)No (use CSMA/CA)

Impact on BER

Undetected collisions at the receiver → corrupted frames → link layer retransmission → increased effective BER and reduced throughput

Solution: RTS/CTS

IEEE 802.11 uses a Request To Send / Clear To Send handshake. Before transmitting, A sends a short RTS to B. B replies with CTS heard by everyone in B's range — including C. C then knows to stay quiet. This "reserves" the channel before the actual data transmission.

A → RTS → B → CTS → (A transmits, C hears CTS and waits)
Technical

Formulas & Decibels

The decibel (dB) system is used throughout wireless communications to express power ratios compactly. You must understand this for any quantitative question.

Why use decibels?

Wireless power spans enormous ranges — from 10 W at a transmitter to 0.000001 mW at a distant receiver. Working with such numbers directly is impractical. The log scale compresses huge ranges into manageable numbers.

Core dB formula

Power ratio in dB = 10 × log₁₀(P₂ / P₁)

P₂ / P₁ = 10^(dB value / 10)

P (dBm) = 10 × log₁₀(P in mW)

Example: 0.001 mW = -30 dBm
Example: 1 mW = 0 dBm
Example: 1000 mW (1W) = 30 dBm

Maximum tolerable path loss

Max path loss (dB) = P_tx (dBm) − P_rx_sensitivity (dBm)

Example: Tx power = 20 dBm, Rx sensitivity = -80 dBm
→ Max path loss = 20 − (−80) = 100 dB

This tells you the maximum distance/obstacle budget the link can tolerate.

Worked example from lecture

Lecture example: 70 dB path loss

Source = 10 W. At distance d₁: 40 dB loss. At distance d₂: 30 dB more loss.

Total path loss = 40 + 30 = 70 dB → power ratio = 10^7 → received power = 10 W / 10,000,000 = 1 μW = 10⁻⁶ W

dBW

100 W = 20 dBW  ·  1 W = 0 dBW  ·  1 mW = −30 dBW

SNR, BER, and Modulation

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and Bit Error Rate (BER) are the two most critical performance metrics in wireless. They are inversely related.

Definitions

SNR (dB) = 10 × log₁₀(Signal Power / Noise Power)

Higher SNR → cleaner signal → lower BER → higher throughput

Rate adaptation — the key wireless technique

Idea: match modulation to current channel quality

As a device moves farther from the base station, SNR drops and BER rises. Rather than failing completely, the system switches to a lower-rate, more robust modulation (e.g., from QAM-256 → BPSK). Speed drops but connection is maintained.

SNR high → use QAM-256 (8 Mbps) → fast but fragile
SNR drops → switch to QAM-16 (4 Mbps) → moderate
SNR very low → switch to BPSK (1 Mbps) → slow but reliable

Common confusions & tricky areas

CSMA/CA
Collision Detection — used in wired Ethernet (802.3). Sender transmits and simultaneously listens. If it hears a collision, it stops and retries.
Collision Avoidance — used in wireless Wi-Fi (802.11). Sender waits for channel to be clear, then waits a random backoff time before transmitting. Cannot detect collisions once started.
Fast fading
Slow fading
Rapid ms-scale power fluctuations from constructive/destructive multipath. Changes with tiny movements (cm scale).
Gradual average power changes as user moves through large-scale obstacles. Changes over seconds/minutes.
Noise (N)
Interference (I)
Random, unavoidable thermal/impulse noise. Always present. Formula: SINR uses N in denominator.
Structured signals from other transmitters (other Wi-Fi, microwaves). Sometimes avoidable by changing frequency.
Attenuation
Fading
Gradual loss of signal power with distance or through matter. Predictable and smooth.
Rapid, random power variations caused by multipath interference. Unpredictable — can be severe even at short range.
SNR increase
BER change
SNR increases when: signal power rises OR noise/interference decreases. Good for communication.
BER decreases as SNR increases (inverse relationship). Higher SNR → fewer bit errors. These move in opposite directions!

Common mistakes on exams

Mistakes to avoid:

1. Saying wireless uses CSMA/CD — it uses CSMA/CA (CA = Collision Avoidance)

2. Confusing dB as an absolute unit — dB always expresses a ratio between two powers

3. Saying multipath always reduces signal — sometimes it increases it (constructive interference)

4. Forgetting hidden terminal problem only applies to wireless, not wired

5. Confusing BER of 10⁻¹² (wired, excellent) with 10⁻⁶ (wireless, common)

Quiz preparation

Quiz questions & strong answers

Click "Show answer" to reveal a model answer for each question.

Q1. Why is wireless bit error rate much higher than wired? Name three specific causes.

Answer: Wireless BER (commonly 10⁻⁶) is far higher than wired (10⁻¹²) because: (1) Signal attenuation — signals weaken with distance and through obstacles, lowering SNR; (2) Multipath fading — reflected copies of the signal arrive with different delays, causing destructive interference or inter-symbol interference; (3) Interference — the shared ISM spectrum means competing devices (other Wi-Fi, microwaves) add noise. Additionally, Doppler shift from mobility and hidden terminal collisions further corrupt data.

Q2. Explain the hidden terminal problem with an example. Why doesn't it occur in wired networks?

Answer: In an A–B–C topology where A and C are both in range of B but not of each other: A checks the channel, hears nothing, and starts transmitting to B. Simultaneously, C also checks, hears nothing, and transmits to B. Both signals collide at B, corrupting the data. Neither A nor C detects this because neither can hear the other's transmission. In wired Ethernet, all nodes share a single physical medium — every transmission is detectable by all nodes with sufficient signal strength, so CSMA/CD works and collisions are caught immediately at the sender.

Q3. A transmitter outputs 30 dBm and the receiver sensitivity is −70 dBm. What is the maximum tolerable path loss? If the path loss at 100m is 60 dB, can this link work?

Answer: Max path loss = P_tx − P_rx = 30 − (−70) = 100 dB. At 100m with 60 dB loss, the received power = 30 − 60 = −30 dBm. Since −30 dBm > −70 dBm (receiver sensitivity), yes — the link works with 40 dB of margin remaining.

Q4. What is rate adaptation and why is it necessary in wireless but not wired networks?

Answer: Rate adaptation dynamically changes the modulation scheme based on current channel quality. As a device moves farther from the AP, SNR drops and BER rises. The system switches from high-rate, high-SNR-requiring modulation (QAM-256 at 8 Mbps) to lower-rate, robust modulation (BPSK at 1 Mbps). This is necessary because wireless channels are time-varying — quality changes with position and environment. Wired channels are stable and do not require this.

Q5. What does 3 dB mean in terms of power? What does a 30 dB path loss represent?

Answer: 3 dB corresponds to a power ratio of approximately 2× (doubling or halving). A 30 dB path loss means the received power is 10³ = 1000 times smaller than the transmitted power. Using the formula: P₂/P₁ = 10^(30/10) = 10³ = 1000.

Q6. Compare CSMA/CD and CSMA/CA. Why can't wireless use collision detection?

Answer: CSMA/CD (Collision Detection, used in 802.3 Ethernet): a sender transmits and simultaneously monitors the channel; if it detects a collision, it stops and waits. CSMA/CA (Collision Avoidance, used in 802.11 Wi-Fi): the sender waits until the channel is idle, then waits an additional random backoff period before transmitting. Collision detection is impossible in wireless because the transmitting device's own signal is orders of magnitude stronger than any incoming signal it could simultaneously detect, effectively masking any collision it might hear.

Q7. What are the three basic propagation mechanisms in wireless? State when each occurs.

Answer: (1) Reflection (λ ≪ D): occurs when the signal wavelength is much smaller than the obstacle — signal bounces off surfaces like buildings and floors. Most common in urban environments. (2) Diffraction (λ ≈ D): occurs when wavelength is comparable to obstacle size — signal bends around edges, enabling coverage around corners. (3) Scattering (λ ≫ D): occurs when the wavelength is much larger than the obstacle (e.g., rain drops, foliage) — signal scatters in many directions, reducing power and causing dispersion.

Q8. Name four types of noise in wireless communications and describe each briefly.

Answer: (1) Thermal noise: random electron motion in any conductor; present always, increases with temperature. (2) Impulse noise: sudden high-energy spikes from lightning or switching equipment; can corrupt many bits. (3) Intermodulation noise: mixing of signals at different frequencies in nonlinear devices, producing unwanted frequency components. (4) Crosstalk: unwanted signal leakage between adjacent channels or cables.
