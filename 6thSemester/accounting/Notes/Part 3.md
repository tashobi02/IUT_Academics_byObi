## 1. What Are Adjusting Entries?

### Simple Explanation

Imagine you run a business and at the end of the month, you look at your records. Some things happened that were never written down — supplies were used up, insurance quietly expired day by day, or a customer paid you in advance for work you haven't done yet. Your books look fine on the surface, but they're not telling the full truth about the month.

**Adjusting entries are the corrections you make at the end of every accounting period to bring your records up to date.**

They don't involve new cash transactions. They are internal corrections that ensure every expense and every revenue is recorded in the right time period — no more, no less.

### Formal Definition

> **Adjusting entries** are journal entries made at the end of an accounting period to ensure that revenues are recognized in the period in which they are earned and expenses are recognized in the period in which they are incurred, in accordance with the **Revenue Recognition Principle** and the **Matching Principle** (GAAP).

### The Two Principles Behind Adjusting Entries

|Principle|What It Says|Why It Matters|
|---|---|---|
|**Revenue Recognition Principle**|Record revenue when it is _earned_, not when cash is received|Prevents recording unearned cash as revenue|
|**Matching Principle**|Match expenses to the same period as the revenue they helped generate|Prevents expenses from appearing in the wrong period|

---

## 2. Why Are They Needed?

### Simple Explanation

The **trial balance** is your first summary of all accounts at the end of the period. But it is often **incomplete and out-of-date** for three specific reasons:

### Three Reasons Adjusting Entries Are Needed

#### Reason 1 — Not Recorded Daily (Inefficiency)

Some things happen every day but are too small or frequent to record daily.

> **Example:** Supplies are used a little every day. No one records "used 3 sheets of paper" each morning. Instead, at month-end, you count what's left and record the total used.

#### Reason 2 — Time-Based Expiration

Some costs expire with the passage of time — they don't happen because of a single transaction.

> **Example:** You paid $600 for a one-year insurance policy. Every single day, a small portion of that coverage expires. You don't record it daily. At month-end, you calculate how much expired and record it.

#### Reason 3 — Unrecorded Items

Some obligations exist that haven't been recorded yet because the bill hasn't arrived.

> **Example:** A utility company provides electricity all month but sends the bill in the following month. The expense is real — it happened this month — but it's not yet in the books.

### The Bottom Line

> **A company must make adjusting entries every time it prepares financial statements.** Without them, assets are overstated, expenses are understated, revenues are wrong, and the financial statements mislead anyone reading them.

## 3. Types of Adjusting Entries

### Simple Explanation

All adjusting entries fall into two big families: **Deferrals** and **Accruals**. Each has two sub-types.

```
ADJUSTING ENTRIES
│
├── DEFERRALS (Cash moved BEFORE work/benefit)
│     ├── Prepaid Expenses     → Paid cash early, benefit comes later
│     └── Unearned Revenues    → Received cash early, service comes later
│
└── ACCRUALS (Cash moves AFTER work/benefit)
      ├── Accrued Revenues     → Earned revenue, cash not received yet
      └── Accrued Expenses     → Incurred expense, cash not paid yet
```

### Overview Table

|Type|What Happened|What Needs Adjusting|
|---|---|---|
|**Prepaid Expense**|Cash paid before expense used|Move used portion from Asset → Expense|
|**Unearned Revenue**|Cash received before service done|Move earned portion from Liability → Revenue|
|**Accrued Revenue**|Service done, cash not received yet|Record revenue earned + receivable|
|**Accrued Expense**|Expense incurred, cash not paid yet|Record expense incurred + payable|

> **Note:** This set of notes focuses on **Deferrals** (Prepaid Expenses and Unearned Revenues) as covered in the uploaded pages.

## 4. Deferrals — Prepaid Expenses

### Simple Explanation

A **prepaid expense** is when a business pays for something _before_ using it.

Think of it like buying a gym membership for a whole year upfront. On January 1 you paid for 12 months. But in January, you've only _used_ one month's worth. The other 11 months are still sitting ahead of you, unused.

- **When paid:** It's an **Asset** (future benefit sitting unused)
- **As time passes / as it gets used:** It becomes an **Expense**

The adjusting entry converts the used portion from Asset → Expense.

### Formal Definition

> **Prepaid expenses** are costs paid in cash and initially recorded as assets because the service or benefit has not yet been consumed. At each period end, an adjusting entry is made to transfer the expired or used portion to an expense account.

### The Pattern — Always the Same

```
Before Adjustment:
  Asset account is OVERSTATED (too high)
  Expense account is UNDERSTATED (too low)

Adjusting Entry:
  Dr. Expense Account      (increase expense)
  Cr. Asset Account        (decrease asset)
```

### 4.1 Supplies

#### Simple Explanation

When a business buys supplies (paper, envelopes, printer cartridges), it records them as an **asset** because they haven't been used yet. At month-end, the accountant physically counts what's still on hand. Whatever is missing was used up — that used-up amount becomes an **expense**.

```
Supplies Used = Opening Balance − Supplies Still on Hand
```

#### Book Example — Pioneer Advertising Agency

**Facts from the trial balance:**

- Advertising Supplies account balance (Oct 31): **$2,500** _(This means $2,500 worth of supplies were purchased on Oct 5)_
- Physical count at Oct 31 reveals: **$1,000 still on hand**
- Therefore, supplies used up in October = $2,500 − $1,000 = **$1,500**

**Step-by-Step Logic:**

| Step | Question                | Answer                                                          |
| ---- | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | What did we start with? | $2,500 in Advertising Supplies (asset)                          |
| 2    | What do we still have?  | $1,000 on hand (confirmed by physical count)                    |
| 3    | What was used?          | $2,500 − $1,000 = $1,500 (this is the expense)                  |
| 4    | What needs adjusting?   | Asset is overstated by $1,500; Expense is understated by $1,500 |

**Adjusting Journal Entry:**

```
Oct. 31   Advertising Supplies Expense     1,500
             Advertising Supplies                    1,500
          (To record supplies used in October)
```

**Why:**

- Advertising Supplies Expense → Expense increases → **Debit**
- Advertising Supplies → Asset decreases → **Credit**

**T-Accounts After Adjustment:**

```
       Advertising Supplies                 Advertising Supplies Expense
  ┌──────────────────────────┐           ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ Debit      │   Credit    │           │  Debit     │   Credit    │
  │ 2,500(10/5)│ 1,500(Adj.) │           │ 1,500(Adj.)│             │
  │            │             │           │            │             │
  │ Bal. 1,000 │             │           │ Bal. 1,500 │             │
  └──────────────────────────┘           └──────────────────────────┘
  (What's still on hand)                 (What was consumed/used)
```

**What Happens If You DON'T Adjust:**

- October expenses understated by $1,500 → Net income overstated by $1,500
- Assets overstated by $1,500 on the balance sheet
- Owner's equity overstated by $1,500

### 4.2 Insurance

#### Simple Explanation

When a business pays for insurance upfront (e.g., one full year), it records it as an asset called **Prepaid Insurance** — because the coverage hasn't been used yet. Every month, one month's worth of coverage "expires." That expired portion is no longer an asset; it becomes an **Insurance Expense**.

```
Monthly Insurance Expense = Total Premium Paid ÷ Number of Months
```

#### Book Example — Pioneer Advertising Agency

**Facts:**

- Paid $600 on Oct 4 for a one-year fire insurance policy (started Oct 1)
- Prepaid Insurance balance in trial balance: **$600**
- Coverage is for 12 months → Monthly cost = $600 ÷ 12 = **$50 per month**
- By Oct 31, one month has passed → **$50 of insurance has expired**

**Step-by-Step Logic:**

|Step|Question|Answer|
|---|---|---|
|1|Total paid?|$600 for 12 months|
|2|Monthly expiry?|$600 ÷ 12 = $50|
|3|How many months passed?|1 month (October)|
|4|Amount to adjust?|$50 expired → move from asset to expense|

**Adjusting Journal Entry:**

```
Oct. 31   Insurance Expense        50
             Prepaid Insurance              50
          (To record insurance expired in October)
```

**Why:**

- Insurance Expense → Expense increases → **Debit**
- Prepaid Insurance → Asset decreases → **Credit**

**T-Accounts After Adjustment:**

```
          Prepaid Insurance                    Insurance Expense
  ┌──────────────────────────┐           ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ Debit      │   Credit    │           │  Debit     │   Credit    │
  │  600(10/4) │  50 (Adj.)  │           │  50 (Adj.) │             │
  │            │             │           │            │             │
  │ Bal. 550   │             │           │ Bal. 50    │             │
  └──────────────────────────┘           └──────────────────────────┘
  (Remaining 11 months coverage)         (1 month expired in Oct)
```

**Interpretation:**

- **$550 remaining** = 11 months of coverage still ahead (still an asset)
- **$50 expense** = the cost of October's coverage (now an expense)

**What Happens If You DON'T Adjust:**

- October expenses understated by $50
- Net income overstated by $50
- Assets and owner's equity overstated by $50

### 4.3 Depreciation

#### Simple Explanation

When a business buys something big and long-lasting — like a computer, a desk, or a machine — it doesn't record the full cost as an expense in year one. Why? Because that asset will be useful for many years, helping earn revenue over its entire life.

**Depreciation** is the process of spreading the cost of a long-lived asset over all the years it is useful. Each month, a small slice of the original cost is moved into an expense account.

> **Think of it like this:** You buy a car for $12,000 that will last 10 years. Instead of recording $12,000 as an expense on day one, you record $1,200 every year ($100/month) as the car gradually gets "used up."

#### Special Account — Accumulated Depreciation (Contra Asset)

Depreciation doesn't reduce the asset account directly. Instead, it uses a special account called **Accumulated Depreciation** — a **contra asset account**.

**Why use a contra account instead of reducing the asset directly?**

Because on the balance sheet, you want to show TWO things:

1. The **original cost** of the asset (what you paid)
2. How much has **worn out** so far (accumulated depreciation)

The difference between these two is called the **Book Value** (also called carrying value or unexpired cost).

```
Book Value = Original Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
```

**Contra asset account rules:**

- It is linked to an asset account
- Its normal balance is **Credit** (opposite of assets which are Debit)
- It reduces the asset's reported value on the balance sheet

#### Book Example — Pioneer Advertising Agency

**Facts:**

- Office Equipment purchased Oct 2: **$5,000**
- Estimated useful life: approximately 10+ years
- Pioneer estimates depreciation at: **$480 per year** → **$40 per month**

**Step-by-Step Logic:**

|Step|Question|Answer|
|---|---|---|
|1|What did the equipment cost?|$5,000|
|2|How much depreciates each year?|$480|
|3|How much depreciates each month?|$480 ÷ 12 = $40|
|4|How many months in October?|1 month|
|5|Amount to record this period?|$40|

**Adjusting Journal Entry:**

```
Oct. 31   Depreciation Expense                          40
             Accumulated Depreciation—Office Equipment          40
          (To record monthly depreciation on office equipment)
```

**Why:**

- Depreciation Expense → Expense increases → **Debit**
- Accumulated Depreciation → Contra Asset increases → **Credit** _(Contra assets increase on the credit side — opposite of regular assets)_

**T-Accounts After Adjustment:**

```
       Office Equipment               Accumulated Depr.—Office Equip.
  ┌──────────────────────────┐       ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ Debit      │   Credit    │       │  Debit     │   Credit    │
  │ 5,000(10/1)│             │       │            │  40 (Adj.)  │
  │            │             │       │            │             │
  │ Bal. 5,000 │             │       │            │ Bal. 40     │
  └──────────────────────────┘       └──────────────────────────┘
  (Original cost — never changes)    (Total wear/use recorded so far)

       Depreciation Expense
  ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │  Debit     │   Credit    │
  │  40 (Adj.) │             │
  │ Bal. 40    │             │
  └──────────────────────────┘
```

**Balance Sheet Presentation:**

```
Assets:
  Office Equipment                          $5,000
  Less: Accumulated Depreciation—Office Equip.  (40)
  Book Value of Office Equipment           $4,960
```

**Key Terms:**

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|**Original Cost**|What was paid for the asset ($5,000)|
|**Accumulated Depreciation**|Total depreciation recorded so far ($40 after Oct)|
|**Book Value**|What the asset is carried at on the books ($4,960)|
|**Market Value**|What the asset could sell for in the marketplace (may differ)|

> **Important:** Depreciation is an **estimate**, not an exact measurement. The exact useful life of an asset is unknown — so accountants estimate it using experience and judgment.

**Summary Table — Accounting for Prepaid Expenses**

|Examples|Reason for Adjustment|Accounts Before Adjustment|Adjusting Entry|
|---|---|---|---|
|Insurance, supplies, advertising, rent, depreciation|Prepaid amounts recorded in asset accounts have been used|Assets overstated; Expenses understated|Dr. Expenses / Cr. Assets|

## 5. Deferrals — Unearned Revenues

### Simple Explanation

**Unearned Revenue** is the exact opposite of a prepaid expense — but from the _other side_ of the transaction.

When a business receives cash _before_ doing the work, it can't call that money "earned" yet. The business now has an **obligation** to perform the service. Until it does, that cash is a **liability** (something owed to the customer).

> **Think of it like this:** A magazine company receives your $120 subscription for a full year. In January, they owe you 12 issues. After delivering one issue, they've earned $10 and still owe you 11 more issues ($110 liability). Each month they deliver, they earn a portion.

```
Cash Received Early → Recorded as LIABILITY (Unearned Revenue)
As service is performed → Move earned portion to REVENUE
```

### Formal Definition

> **Unearned revenues** are cash received and recorded as liabilities before the related revenue is earned. At period end, an adjusting entry is made to recognize the portion of revenue that has been earned by transferring it from the liability account to a revenue account.

### The Pattern — Always the Same

```
Before Adjustment:
  Liability account is OVERSTATED (too high)
  Revenue account is UNDERSTATED (too low)

Adjusting Entry:
  Dr. Unearned Revenue     (decrease liability — obligation fulfilled)
  Cr. Service Revenue      (increase revenue — now earned)
```

### Book Example — Pioneer Advertising Agency

**Facts:**

- Oct 2: Received $1,200 from R. Knox for advertising services to be completed by Dec 31
- This was recorded as: Cash Dr $1,200 / Unearned Revenue Cr $1,200
- Unearned Revenue balance in Oct 31 trial balance: **$1,200**
- Analysis at Oct 31 reveals: **$400 of that work was actually completed in October**
- The remaining $800 is still owed as future service

**Step-by-Step Logic:**

|Step|Question|Answer|
|---|---|---|
|1|How much was received upfront?|$1,200 (all recorded as liability)|
|2|How much service was performed in Oct?|$400 worth|
|3|What must be adjusted?|$400 moves from liability → earned revenue|
|4|What remains as liability?|$1,200 − $400 = $800 (still owed to Knox)|

**Adjusting Journal Entry:**

```
Oct. 31   Unearned Revenue         400
             Service Revenue                400
          (To record revenue earned for services provided in October)
```

**Why:**

- Unearned Revenue → Liability decreases → **Debit** _(liabilities decrease on the debit side)_
- Service Revenue → Revenue increases → **Credit**

**T-Accounts After Adjustment:**

```
          Unearned Revenue                    Service Revenue
  ┌──────────────────────────┐       ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ Debit      │   Credit    │       │  Debit     │   Credit    │
  │ 400 (Adj.) │ 1,200(10/2) │       │            │10,000(Bal.) │
  │            │             │       │            │  400 (Adj.) │
  │            │ Bal. 800    │       │            │             │
  └──────────────────────────┘       └──────────────────────────┘
  ($800 still owed to Knox)          ($10,400 total revenue in Oct)
```

**Interpretation:**

- **$800 remaining liability** = still owe Knox advertising work in Nov/Dec
- **$400 new revenue** = earned this month; now properly in income
- **Total Service Revenue = $10,400** ($10,000 original + $400 adjustment)

**What Happens If You DON'T Adjust:**

- October revenues understated by $400
- Net income understated by $400
- Liabilities overstated by $400 on the balance sheet
- Owner's equity understated by $400

**Summary Table — Accounting for Unearned Revenues**

|Examples|Reason for Adjustment|Accounts Before Adjustment|Adjusting Entry|
|---|---|---|---|
|Rent received in advance, magazine subscriptions, airline tickets, customer deposits|Cash received before service performed — liability recorded|Liabilities overstated; Revenues understated|Dr. Unearned Revenue / Cr. Revenue|

## 6. What Is the Adjusted Trial Balance?

### Simple Explanation

After making all adjusting entries in the journal and posting them to T-accounts, you prepare a **new trial balance** — this time with all the updated balances. This is called the **Adjusted Trial Balance**.

Think of it like this:

- The **Unadjusted Trial Balance** = your first draft with mistakes still in it
- **Adjusting Entries** = the corrections you make
- The **Adjusted Trial Balance** = the clean, corrected final version ready to use for financial statements

### Formal Definition

> The **Adjusted Trial Balance** is a list of all accounts and their balances **after** adjusting entries have been recorded and posted. It is prepared to verify that total debits still equal total credits, and it serves as the basis for preparing the formal financial statements.

### Why Is It Needed?

1. **Accuracy** — confirms that the books are balanced after all corrections
2. **Foundation** — the adjusted trial balance is the direct source for preparing:
    - The Income Statement
    - The Owner's Equity Statement
    - The Balance Sheet

### How Is It Prepared?

**Step 1:** Start with the Unadjusted Trial Balance (all account balances before adjustments)

**Step 2:** Record all adjusting journal entries in the General Journal

**Step 3:** Post each adjusting entry to the relevant T-accounts (update balances)

**Step 4:** List every account with its **new updated balance** in the Adjusted Trial Balance

**Step 5:** Verify that Total Debits = Total Credits

### How It Differs from Unadjusted Trial Balance

|Feature|Unadjusted Trial Balance|Adjusted Trial Balance|
|---|---|---|
|**Timing**|Before adjusting entries|After adjusting entries|
|**Completeness**|Incomplete — some revenues/expenses missing|Complete — all revenues and expenses included|
|**Accuracy**|May overstate/understate accounts|Accurate and GAAP-compliant|
|**Used for**|Starting point for adjustments|Preparing financial statements|
|**New accounts**|Only original accounts|May include new accounts (e.g., Depreciation Expense, Insurance Expense)|

### Connecting the Three Steps

```
UNADJUSTED             ADJUSTING              ADJUSTED
TRIAL BALANCE    +     ENTRIES         =     TRIAL BALANCE
─────────────          ─────────              ────────────────
Cash: $15,200          No change              Cash: $15,200
Adv. Supplies: $2,500  Cr. $1,500             Adv. Supplies: $1,000
Prepaid Ins.: $600     Cr. $50                Prepaid Ins.: $550
Office Equip.: $5,000  No change              Office Equip.: $5,000
                       Dr. Accum. Dep. $40    Accum. Dep.: $40
Unearned Rev.: $1,200  Dr. $400               Unearned Rev.: $800
Service Rev.: $10,000  Cr. $400               Service Rev.: $10,400
                       Dr. Adv. Sup. Exp $1,500  Adv. Sup. Exp: $1,500
                       Dr. Ins. Exp. $50      Ins. Exp: $50
                       Dr. Dep. Exp. $40      Dep. Exp: $40
```

## 7. DO IT Practice Problem — Hammond, Inc. (Full Solution)

### Problem Statement

**Hammond, Inc.** — Ledger on March 31, 2010, before adjusting entries:

|Account|Debit|Credit|
|---|---|---|
|Prepaid Insurance|$3,600||
|Office Supplies|$2,800||
|Office Equipment|$25,000||
|Accumulated Depreciation—Office Equipment||$5,000|
|Unearned Revenue||$9,200|

**Additional Information (Analysis of Accounts):**

1. Insurance expires at the rate of **$100 per month**
2. Supplies on hand total **$800**
3. Office equipment depreciates **$200 a month**
4. **One-half** of the unearned revenue was earned in March

**Required:** Prepare the adjusting entries for the month of March.

---

### Full Solution — Entry by Entry

---

#### Adjustment 1 — Insurance Expense

**Understanding the situation:**

- Prepaid Insurance balance = $3,600 (this is the total unused insurance sitting as an asset)
- Each month, $100 of that coverage expires and becomes an expense
- In March: $100 has expired → must move $100 from asset to expense

**Logic Check:**

|Before Adjustment|Problem|
|---|---|
|Prepaid Insurance overstated by $100|$100 of coverage was consumed in March|
|Insurance Expense understated by $100|This cost was never recorded|

**Adjusting Entry:**

```
Mar. 31   Insurance Expense         100
             Prepaid Insurance               100
          (To record insurance expired in March)
```

**Why:**

- Insurance Expense increases → **Debit $100**
- Prepaid Insurance (asset) decreases → **Credit $100**

**After Adjustment:**

```
       Prepaid Insurance                    Insurance Expense
  Before: $3,600                        Before: $0
  Credit:   $100                        Debit:  $100
  After:  $3,500                        After:  $100
```

> **$3,500 remaining** = still-unused insurance for future months **$100 expense** = the cost of March's coverage, properly recorded

---

#### Adjustment 2 — Office Supplies Expense

**Understanding the situation:**

- Office Supplies balance = $2,800 (total supplies purchased, recorded as asset)
- Physical count reveals only $800 still on hand
- Supplies used up in March = $2,800 − $800 = **$2,000**

**Calculation:**

```
Supplies Used = Balance in Account − Supplies Still on Hand
             = $2,800 − $800
             = $2,000
```

**Logic Check:**

|Before Adjustment|Problem|
|---|---|
|Office Supplies overstated by $2,000|$2,000 of supplies were consumed|
|Office Supplies Expense understated by $2,000|This cost was never recorded|

**Adjusting Entry:**

```
Mar. 31   Office Supplies Expense    2,000
             Office Supplies                  2,000
          (To record supplies used in March)
```

**Why:**

- Office Supplies Expense increases → **Debit $2,000**
- Office Supplies (asset) decreases → **Credit $2,000**

**After Adjustment:**

```
       Office Supplies                    Office Supplies Expense
  Before: $2,800                        Before: $0
  Credit:  $2,000                       Debit:  $2,000
  After:    $800                        After:  $2,000
```

> **$800 remaining** = supplies still on hand (confirmed by physical count) **$2,000 expense** = the cost of all supplies consumed in March

---

#### Adjustment 3 — Depreciation on Office Equipment

**Understanding the situation:**

- Office Equipment cost = $25,000
- Already accumulated: $5,000 of prior depreciation (Accumulated Depreciation account)
- Monthly depreciation = **$200**
- Must record $200 of depreciation for March

**Logic Check:**

|Before Adjustment|Problem|
|---|---|
|Depreciation Expense $0|$200 of equipment cost expired in March|
|Accumulated Depreciation $5,000|Doesn't include March yet — needs $200 added|

**Adjusting Entry:**

```
Mar. 31   Depreciation Expense                          200
             Accumulated Depreciation—Office Equipment          200
          (To record monthly depreciation on office equipment)
```

**Why:**

- Depreciation Expense increases → **Debit $200**
- Accumulated Depreciation (contra asset) increases → **Credit $200**

**After Adjustment:**

```
   Office Equipment (unchanged)             Accumulated Depreciation
   Balance: $25,000                         Before: $5,000
                                            Credit:   $200
                                            After:  $5,200

   Depreciation Expense
   Before: $0
   Debit:  $200
   After:  $200
```

**Balance Sheet Presentation After Adjustment:**

```
Office Equipment                              $25,000
Less: Accumulated Depreciation—Office Equip.  (5,200)
Book Value of Office Equipment               $19,800
```

> **$19,800** = what the equipment is currently "worth" on the books This decreases by $200 every month as the equipment depreciates

---

#### Adjustment 4 — Unearned Revenue

**Understanding the situation:**

- Unearned Revenue balance = $9,200 (cash received in advance — all recorded as liability)
- One-half was earned in March = $9,200 × ½ = **$4,600**
- The other half ($4,600) is still owed as future service → remains a liability

**Calculation:**

```
Revenue Earned in March = $9,200 × (1/2) = $4,600
Liability Remaining     = $9,200 − $4,600 = $4,600
```

**Logic Check:**

|Before Adjustment|Problem|
|---|---|
|Unearned Revenue overstated by $4,600|$4,600 of service was actually performed in March|
|Service Revenue understated by $4,600|This earned revenue was never recorded|

**Adjusting Entry:**

```
Mar. 31   Unearned Revenue          4,600
             Service Revenue                  4,600
          (To record revenue earned for services provided in March)
```

**Why:**

- Unearned Revenue (liability) decreases → **Debit $4,600**
- Service Revenue increases → **Credit $4,600**

**After Adjustment:**

```
        Unearned Revenue                    Service Revenue
  Before: $9,200 (Credit)              Before: (whatever it was)
  Debit:   $4,600                      Credit: +$4,600 added
  After:   $4,600 (Credit)             After:  Increased by $4,600
```

> **$4,600 remaining liability** = service still owed to customers in future months **$4,600 new revenue** = properly recognized as earned in March

### Complete Solution Summary — All Four Adjusting Entries

```
HAMMOND, INC.
Adjusting Journal Entries
Month Ended March 31, 2010

  1.  Insurance Expense                    100
         Prepaid Insurance                          100
      (To record insurance expired in March)

  2.  Office Supplies Expense            2,000

      (To record supplies used in March)

  3.  Depreciation Expense                200
         Accumulated Depreciation—Office Equip.    200
      (To record monthly depreciation)

  4.  Unearned Revenue                  4,600
         Service Revenue                          4,600
      (To record revenue earned for services provided)
```

|Entry|Account Affected|Income Statement|Balance Sheet|
|---|---|---|---|
|1 (Insurance)|Insurance Expense ↑ $100|Expenses ↑ → Net Income ↓|Prepaid Insurance (Asset) ↓|
|2 (Supplies)|Supplies Expense ↑ $2,000|Expenses ↑ → Net Income ↓|Office Supplies (Asset) ↓|

|4 (Unearned)|Service Revenue ↑ $4,600|Revenue ↑ → Net Income ↑|Unearned Revenue (Liability) ↓|

## 8. Quick Reference Summary

### The Master Logic

```
DEFERRALS — Cash moved BEFORE work

  PREPAID EXPENSES                    UNEARNED REVENUES
  (You paid early)                    (You were paid early)
  Asset → Expense                     Liability → Revenue

  Dr. Expense                         Dr. Unearned Revenue
  Cr. Asset                           Cr. Revenue

  Asset was overstated.               Liability was overstated.
  Expense was understated.            Revenue was understated.
```

### What If You Skip Adjusting Entries?

|Skipped Entry|Effect on Net Income|Effect on Assets|Effect on Liabilities|
|---|---|---|---|
|Prepaid Expense (e.g., insurance)|**Overstated**|**Overstated**|No effect|
|Depreciation|**Overstated**|**Overstated** (book value too high)|No effect|
|Supplies used|**Overstated**|**Overstated**|No effect|
|Unearned Revenue|**Understated**|No effect|**Overstated**|

### The Adjusting Entry Never Touches Cash

> This is the most important rule to remember: **adjusting entries never involve Cash.** They move amounts between asset/liability accounts and expense/revenue accounts. If you see Cash in an adjusting entry — it is wrong.

### Three-Statement Flow After Adjusted Trial Balance

```
ADJUSTED TRIAL BALANCE
        │
        ├──→ INCOME STATEMENT
        │     (Revenue − Expenses = Net Income)
        │               │
        │               ↓
        ├──→ OWNER'S EQUITY STATEMENT
        │     (Opening Capital + Investment + Net Income − Drawings)
        │               │
        │               ↓
        └──→ BALANCE SHEET
              (Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity)
```
