# Journal Entries — A Complete Beginner's Guide

---

## 1. Journal Entry Basics

### What is a journal entry?

A journal entry is the **very first place a financial transaction gets recorded** in accounting. Before anything goes into a spreadsheet, report, or balance sheet — it starts here, as a journal entry.

Think of it like a diary for the business. Every time money moves, every time something is bought, sold, owed, or paid — you write it down in the journal, in a specific format, so nothing is ever lost or untracked.

> **Formal definition:** A journal entry is a chronological record of a business transaction, showing which accounts are affected, by how much, and in which direction (debit or credit).

---

### Why do we use journal entries?

Because real businesses have **hundreds or thousands of transactions** happening every month. Without a structured system to record each one, you'd have chaos. Journal entries solve this by:

- Creating a permanent, dated record of every transaction
- Showing **exactly** which accounts changed and by how much
- Ensuring the accounting equation stays balanced after every single entry
- Providing an audit trail — anyone can trace back exactly what happened and when

---

### How a journal entry works — step by step

Every time a transaction happens, ask yourself these three questions:

**Step 1 — What accounts are affected?** Every transaction touches at least two accounts. Identify them.

**Step 2 — How are they affected?** Does each account go up or down?

**Step 3 — Which side do they go on?** Based on the account type, decide if it's a debit or credit.

---

### Format of a journal entry

```
Date        Account Name               Debit       Credit
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Sep 1       Cash                       15,000
               Owner's Capital                     15,000
            (Owner invested cash into the business)
```

**Rules of format:**

- The **debited account** always goes on the **top-left**
- The **credited account** goes **below, indented to the right**
- Every entry must have a **brief description** (called a narration)
- **Debits always equal credits** — without exception

## 2. Debit and Credit

### What do they actually mean?

This is the #1 source of confusion for beginners — because in everyday life, "debit" means money leaving your bank account and "credit" means money coming in. **That is NOT what they mean in accounting.**

In accounting, debit and credit are simply **the two sides of any account:**

```
         Any Account
   ┌─────────────────────┐
   │   Debit  │  Credit  │
   │  (Left)  │ (Right)  │
   └─────────────────────┘
```

That's it. Debit = left side. Credit = right side. They are directional labels, not value judgments. Neither is inherently "good" or "bad."

What makes them powerful is what each side **means for different account types** — and that's where the rules come in.

---

### How accounts are categorized

Every account in accounting falls into one of five types:

|Account Type|Examples|
|---|---|
|Asset|Cash, Equipment, Supplies, Accounts Receivable|
|Liability|Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Unearned Revenue|
|Owner's Equity|Owner's Capital, Drawings, Revenue, Expenses|
|Revenue|Service Revenue, Sales Revenue|
|Expense|Rent, Salaries, Utilities, Advertising|

Each type has a **natural side** — the side that increases it. This is the key to everything.

---

### Why debit/credit don't always mean increase/decrease

A debit **increases** some accounts and **decreases** others. Same with credit. It depends entirely on what **type** of account you're dealing with.

This feels confusing at first, but there's a deep logic behind it — which brings us to the rules.

---

## 3. The Accounting Rules — With Logic, Not Just Memory

The entire debit/credit system exists to keep one equation permanently true:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
```

The **left side** of this equation (Assets) increases with **debits**. The **right side** (Liabilities + Equity) increases with **credits**.

That's the master logic. Now let's walk through each rule.

---

### Assets — Debit to increase, Credit to decrease

Assets live on the **left side** of the accounting equation. So they naturally increase on the **left side of the T-account**, which is the debit side.

**Logic:** When you gain an asset (more cash, more equipment), the left side of the equation grows. To keep it growing correctly, you record that on the left (debit) side.

```
Example: Receive $5,000 cash
→ Cash (asset) goes UP → Debit Cash $5,000 ✓
```

When an asset decreases, you record it on the opposite side (credit) to reduce it back.

```
Example: Pay $500 rent in cash
→ Cash (asset) goes DOWN → Credit Cash $500 ✓
```

---

### Liabilities — Credit to increase, Debit to decrease

Liabilities live on the **right side** of the accounting equation. So they increase on the **right side of the T-account**, which is the credit side.

**Logic:** When you take on a new debt, the right side of the equation grows. To reflect that, you record it on the right (credit) side.

```
Example: Buy supplies on credit, owe $1,000
→ Accounts Payable (liability) goes UP → Credit Accounts Payable $1,000 ✓
```

When you pay off a debt, the liability shrinks — so you record it on the opposite side (debit).

```
Example: Pay off the $1,000 you owed
→ Accounts Payable goes DOWN → Debit Accounts Payable $1,000 ✓
```

---

### Owner's Equity — Credit to increase, Debit to decrease

Equity also lives on the **right side** of the equation, so it follows the same pattern as liabilities.

**Logic:** When the owner's stake in the business grows (from profit or investment), the right side of the equation grows. Record on the right (credit) side.

```
Example: Owner invests $10,000
→ Owner's Capital (equity) goes UP → Credit Owner's Capital $10,000 ✓
```

---

### Revenue — Credit to increase

Revenue **increases** owner's equity (profit belongs to the owner). Since equity increases on the credit side, revenue also increases on the **credit side.**

**Logic:** Earning money makes the business worth more → owner's equity grows → right side of equation → credit.

```
Example: Earn $2,000 from services
→ Service Revenue goes UP → Credit Service Revenue $2,000 ✓
```

---

### Expenses — Debit to increase

Expenses **decrease** owner's equity (costs eat into profit). Since equity decreases on the debit side, expenses increase on the **debit side** — they represent the reduction of equity.

**Logic:** Spending money to operate reduces the business's net worth → equity shrinks → opposite of credit side → debit.

```
Example: Pay $600 rent
→ Rent Expense goes UP → Debit Rent Expense $600 ✓
```

> Think of it this way: recording an expense on the debit side is your way of saying "equity just took a hit here."

---

### The Master Summary Table

|Account Type|Increases with|Decreases with|Normal Balance|
|---|---|---|---|
|Asset|**Debit**|Credit|Debit|
|Liability|**Credit**|Debit|Credit|
|Owner's Capital|**Credit**|Debit|Credit|
|Revenue|**Credit**|Debit|Credit|
|Expense|**Debit**|Credit|Debit|
|Drawings|**Debit**|Credit|Debit|

## 4. Specific Terms — Explained With Logic

### Unearned Service Revenue is a Liability

**Scenario:** A client pays your business $3,000 in advance for work you haven't done yet.

Cash came in — great. But you haven't _earned_ it yet. If you never do the work, you'd have to give it back. You have an **obligation** to either perform the service or refund the money.

That obligation = a **liability**.

```
Journal Entry:
Cash                    3,000
   Unearned Revenue              3,000
(Received payment before service is performed)
```

Later, when you actually perform the service, you _earn_ the revenue — and the liability disappears:

```
Unearned Revenue        3,000
   Service Revenue               3,000
(Service now performed — revenue recognized)
```

> The core logic: money received ≠ money earned. Until the work is done, it's a debt of service.

---

### Prepaid Insurance is an Asset

**Scenario:** You pay $1,200 for a full year of insurance coverage upfront.

You paid cash, but you didn't lose that value — you bought **12 months of future protection**. That future benefit sitting on your shelf, unused, is an **asset**.

```
Journal Entry:
Prepaid Insurance       1,200
   Cash                          1,200
(Paid insurance in advance for future coverage)
```

Each month, as one month of coverage gets used up, a portion moves from asset to expense:

```
Insurance Expense         100
   Prepaid Insurance               100
(One month of insurance coverage consumed)
```

> The core logic: you paid for something you haven't used yet. Future benefit = asset. Used-up benefit = expense.

---

### Supplies — Asset when purchased, Expense when used

**Scenario:** Buy $500 of printer paper and pens.

You haven't used them yet. They're sitting in the supply closet, available for future use. That's a future benefit → **asset**.

```
Journal Entry:
Supplies                  500
   Cash                            500
(Purchased office supplies for future use)
```

Once you use them up, they become an expense:

```
Supplies Expense          500
   Supplies                        500
(Supplies consumed during the period)
```

> Asset first. Expense later. The moment of transformation is when they're used.

---

### Drawings — Debit because they decrease equity

**Scenario:** The owner takes $1,000 from the business for personal use.

This isn't a business expense — the business didn't buy anything useful. The owner just took money out for themselves. This reduces the owner's stake in the business (equity decreases).

Since equity decreases on the **debit side**, Drawings is debited.

```
Journal Entry:
Owner's Drawings        1,000
   Cash                          1,000
(Owner withdrew cash for personal use)
```

> Drawings are not expenses. They don't appear on the income statement. They reduce equity directly, which is why they always sit on the debit side.

---

### Taking out cash — Credit in relevant context

When the business **spends** or **gives away** cash, cash is going down. Since cash is an asset, and assets decrease on the **credit side** — you credit cash.

```
Example: Pay $800 salary to an employee
→ Salary Expense goes UP → Debit
→ Cash goes DOWN → Credit

Journal Entry:
Salaries Expense          800
   Cash                            800
```

> "Crediting cash" simply means cash decreased. It's not bad — it's just accounting language for the asset going down.

---

## 5. Conceptual Intuition

### Simple way to remember Debit and Credit

Use this memory anchor:

```
   LEFT side of equation → LEFT side of T-account → DEBIT
   (Assets)

   RIGHT side of equation → RIGHT side of T-account → CREDIT
   (Liabilities + Equity)
```

Or use this phrase:

> **"DEA — dividends, expenses, assets increase with Debits."** **"LER — liabilities, equity, revenue increase with Credits."**

Everything else is just the reverse of these.

---

### How the equation stays balanced

Every journal entry touches **at least two accounts**, and the total debits **always equal** total credits. This is called **double-entry bookkeeping**, and it's the reason the accounting equation never breaks.

**Example — buy a $3,000 laptop with cash:**

```
Equipment (asset)   +3,000  → Debit   (asset increases)
Cash (asset)        −3,000  → Credit  (asset decreases)
```

Left side of equation: assets went up $3,000 and down $3,000 → net change = zero. Equation stays balanced. ✓

**Example — take a $5,000 loan:**

```
Cash (asset)        +5,000  → Debit   (asset increases)
Loan Payable (liab) +5,000  → Credit  (liability increases)
```

Left side up $5,000. Right side up $5,000. Balanced. ✓

**The equation is self-correcting** — as long as debits equal credits in every entry, it can never go out of balance.

## Transaction 1 — Oct. 1 | Owner Invests Cash

```

   Owner's Capital      301              10,000

```

### What happened?
The owner put **$10,000 of personal money** into the business to get it started. This is called the **initial capital investment.**

### Why this journal entry?

| Account | Type | Direction | Rule | Side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | Asset | Increases ↑ | Assets increase = Debit | **Debit** |
| Owner's Capital | Equity | Increases ↑ | Equity increases = Credit | **Credit** |

### Logic:
The business now *owns* $10,000 cash → asset goes up → debit.
That cash didn't come from nowhere — it came from the owner's personal pocket, so the owner now has a $10,000 stake in the business → equity goes up → credit.

```
Equation check:
Assets +10,000 = Liabilities 0 + Equity +10,000  ✓

## Transaction 2 — Oct. 1 | Buy Equipment on Credit (Note)

```
Equipment               157     5,000
   Notes Payable        200              5,000
(Issued 3-month, 12% note for office equipment)
```

### What happened?
The business bought **office equipment worth $5,000** but didn't pay cash. Instead, it signed a **formal written promise** (a Notes Payable) to repay in 3 months at 12% interest.

### Why this journal entry?

| Account | Type | Direction | Rule | Side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment | Asset | Increases ↑ | Assets increase = Debit | **Debit** |
| Notes Payable | Liability | Increases ↑ | Liabilities increase = Credit | **Credit** |

### Logic:
A new asset (equipment) arrived → asset up → debit.
No cash was paid — instead, a debt was created. That obligation to repay is a **liability** → liability up → credit.

> **Notes Payable vs Accounts Payable:** Both are debts, but Notes Payable comes with a signed, formal document specifying an interest rate and repayment date. Accounts Payable is more informal (a simple bill). Here the note is 3 months at 12% annual interest — meaning by month 3, interest will also be owed.

```
Equation check:
Assets +5,000 = Liabilities +5,000 + Equity 0  ✓
```

## Transaction 3 — Oct. 2 | Receive Cash in Advance for Future Service

```
Cash                    101     1,200
   Unearned Service Revenue  209       1,200
(Received cash from R. Knox for future service)
```

### What happened?
A client named R. Knox paid **$1,200 upfront** for services the business hasn't performed yet.

### Why this journal entry?

| Account | Type | Direction | Rule | Side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | Asset | Increases ↑ | Assets increase = Debit | **Debit** |
| Unearned Service Revenue | Liability | Increases ↑ | Liabilities increase = Credit | **Credit** |

### Logic:
Cash came in → asset up → debit. ✓

Later, when the service is actually performed:
```

   Service Revenue                   1,200
(Service performed — revenue now earned)

## The One Insight That Connects Everything

Every single journal entry in this page is an expression of the same truth:

> **Something was received → something was given, owed, or consumed in return.**

When cash came in from the owner → equity was given in return.
When equipment arrived → a debt was created in return.
When rent was paid → cash was given in return for space used.
When Knox paid in advance → an obligation to serve was created in return.

Accounting is just the system that captures **both sides of every exchange**, so that nothing is ever unaccounted for and the equation never breaks.


# T-Accounts — Complete Beginner's Guide

---

## 1. What is a T-Account?

### Simple Definition

A T-account is a simplified, visual representation of a single account in the accounting system. It looks exactly like the letter **T** — which is where the name comes from.

Every account in a business — Cash, Equipment, Rent Expense, Owner's Capital — has its own T-account. Think of each T-account as a **personal scoreboard** for that one account, tracking every increase and decrease that ever happens to it.

---

### Structure

```
         Account Name
    ┌─────────────────────┐
    │   Debit  │  Credit  │
    │  (Left)  │ (Right)  │
    │          │          │
    │          │          │
    └─────────────────────┘
```

That's it. Two sides. Left is always **Debit**. Right is always **Credit**. Every entry ever made to an account goes on one of these two sides — never anywhere else.

---

### Why T-Accounts are Used

Journal entries tell you **what happened** in a transaction. But imagine you want to know: _"How much cash does the business have right now?"_ You'd have to read through every single journal entry and manually track every cash movement. That's chaotic.

T-accounts solve this by giving **each account its own dedicated space.** All debits to Cash go on the left of the Cash T-account. All credits go on the right. At any moment, you subtract one side from the other and get the **current balance** instantly.

> **Journal entry = the story of a transaction** **T-account = the running scoreboard of a single account**

Both are necessary. One without the other gives you an incomplete picture.

---

## 2. Relationship with Journal Entries

### The Three-Step Flow

Every financial event in a business follows this exact path:

```
  STEP 1              STEP 2                STEP 3
─────────────    ──────────────────    ─────────────────
 Transaction  →   Journal Entry    →    T-Accounts
─────────────    ──────────────────    ─────────────────
 Something        Record WHAT           Post each debit
 happens          happened, which       and credit into
 in real life     accounts, and         its own account's
                  which direction       running scoreboard
```

This process of moving an entry from the journal into the T-accounts is called **posting.**

---

### How Posting Works

When you post a journal entry, you simply take each line of the entry and write the amount on the correct side of the correct T-account.

```
Rule:
If an account is DEBITED in the journal → write amount on LEFT side of its T-account
If an account is CREDITED in the journal → write amount on RIGHT side of its T-account
```

Simple. Mechanical. You're just copying numbers to the right place.

---

### Why Both Tools are Needed

|Tool|Purpose|Answers the Question|
|---|---|---|
|Journal Entry|Chronological record of transactions|"What happened on this date?"|
|T-Account|Running balance of one account|"What is the total in this account?"|

A journal entry shows the full picture of one transaction. A T-account shows the full history of one account.

Together, they give you **complete control** over the financial data of a business.

## 3. Step-by-Step Examples

### Example 1 — Owner Invests $15,000 Cash

**Transaction:** On Oct 1, the owner puts $15,000 of personal cash into the business.

#### Step 1 — Journal Entry

```
Date       Account               Ref.    Debit     Credit
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Oct 1      Cash                  101    15,000
              Owner's Capital    301              15,000
           (Owner invested cash in business)
```

#### Step 2 — Identify What Gets Posted

- Cash is **debited** $15,000 → goes on **LEFT** side of Cash T-account
- Owner's Capital is **credited** $15,000 → goes on **RIGHT** side of Owner's Capital T-account

#### Step 3 — Post to T-Accounts

```
           Cash (101)                    Owner's Capital (301)
    ┌──────────────────────┐           ┌──────────────────────┐
    │ Debit    │  Credit   │           │ Debit    │  Credit   │
    │──────────│───────────│           │──────────│───────────│
    │  15,000  │           │           │          │  15,000   │
    │          │           │           │          │           │
    └──────────────────────┘           └──────────────────────┘
    Balance: $15,000 (Debit)           Balance: $15,000 (Credit)
```

**Why?** Cash is an asset → increases on the debit (left) side → post $15,000 to the left. Owner's Capital is equity → increases on the credit (right) side → post $15,000 to the right.

---

### Example 2 — Buy Equipment for $7,000 Cash

**Transaction:** Oct 2 — Business buys office equipment paying $7,000 cash.

#### Step 1 — Journal Entry

```
Date       Account               Ref.    Debit     Credit
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Oct 2      Equipment             157     7,000
              Cash               101               7,000
           (Purchased equipment for cash)
```

#### Step 2 — Identify What Gets Posted

- Equipment is **debited** $7,000 → LEFT side of Equipment T-account
- Cash is **credited** $7,000 → RIGHT side of Cash T-account

#### Step 3 — Post to T-Accounts

```
        Equipment (157)                      Cash (101)
  ┌──────────────────────┐         ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ Debit    │  Credit   │         │  Debit     │   Credit    │
  │──────────│───────────│         │────────────│─────────────│
  │   7,000  │           │         │   15,000   │    7,000    │
  │          │           │         │  (Oct 1)   │   (Oct 2)   │
  └──────────────────────┘         └──────────────────────────┘
  Balance: $7,000 (Debit)          Balance: $8,000 (Debit)
```

> Notice: The Cash T-account now has **two entries** — $15,000 from Example 1 (left) and $7,000 from this transaction (right). The running balance is $15,000 − $7,000 = **$8,000.**

This is exactly why T-accounts are powerful — they accumulate every movement in one account and always show you the current balance.

### Example 3 — Pay Rent $900, Earn Revenue $3,500

**Transaction A:** Oct 3 — Paid $900 rent in cash. **Transaction B:** Oct 5 — Performed services, received $3,500 cash.

#### Journal Entries

```
Date       Account                   Ref.   Debit    Credit
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Oct 3      Rent Expense              729      900
              Cash                   101               900
           (Paid October rent)

Oct 5      Cash                      101    3,500
              Service Revenue        400             3,500
           (Received cash for services performed)
```

#### Post All Four Lines to T-Accounts

```
       Rent Expense (729)                  Service Revenue (400)
  ┌──────────────────────┐            ┌──────────────────────┐
  │ Debit    │  Credit   │            │ Debit    │  Credit   │
  │──────────│───────────│            │──────────│───────────│
  │    900   │           │            │          │   3,500   │
  └──────────────────────┘            └──────────────────────┘
  Balance: $900 (Debit)               Balance: $3,500 (Credit)


                      Cash (101) — Running Total
          ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
          │         Debit       │      Credit        │
          │─────────────────────│────────────────────│
          │  15,000  (Oct 1)    │   7,000  (Oct 2)   │
          │   3,500  (Oct 5)    │     900  (Oct 3)   │
          │                     │                    │
          │  Total: 18,500      │   Total: 7,900     │
          └──────────────────────────────────────────┘
          Balance: 18,500 − 7,900 = $10,600 (Debit)
```

## 4. Understanding Balances

### How to Find the Ending Balance

At any point, calculate the balance of a T-account like this:

```
Step 1: Add up all amounts on the LEFT (debit) side
Step 2: Add up all amounts on the RIGHT (credit) side
Step 3: Subtract the smaller side from the larger side
Step 4: The balance sits on whichever side is LARGER
```

**Example — using the Cash T-account above:**

```
Left (Debit) total:   15,000 + 3,500 = 18,500
Right (Credit) total:  7,000 + 900   =  7,900

Difference: 18,500 − 7,900 = 10,600
Larger side = Left (Debit)
∴ Cash balance = $10,600 DEBIT balance
```

---

### Debit Balance vs Credit Balance

Every account has a **normal balance** — the side it naturally sits on when healthy. This is determined by the account type:

```
                    DEBIT BALANCE              CREDIT BALANCE
                 (Left side is bigger)       (Right side is bigger)
                ┌─────────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────────┐
Normal for →    │ Assets              │    │ Liabilities         │
                │ Expenses            │    │ Owner's Capital     │
                │ Drawings            │    │ Revenue             │
                └─────────────────────┘    └─────────────────────┘
```

> **Warning sign:** If an account has a balance on the _opposite_ side of its normal balance, something is likely wrong — either an error was made or the account went negative. For example, if Cash shows a credit balance, that would mean the business has negative cash — which should be investigated immediately.

## 5. Intuition & Memory Tricks

### The Accounting Equation as Your Map

The single most powerful memory tool is to anchor everything to the equation:

```
Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Owner's Equity
 LEFT           RIGHT            RIGHT
  ↓               ↓                ↓
Debit side    Credit side      Credit side
increases it  increases it     increases it
```

Everything that lives on the **left** of the equation (Assets) increases on the **left** of its T-account (Debit). Everything on the **right** (Liabilities, Equity) increases on the **right** (Credit).

Once you feel this, the rules stop being memorization and become pure logic.

---

### The DEA-LER Trick

```
  DEA  →  Debit increases these:
  ─────────────────────────────
  D rawings
  E xpenses
  A ssets


  LER  →  Credit increases these:
  ─────────────────────────────
  L iabilities
  E quity (Owner's Capital)
  R evenue
```

If you remember **DEA-LER**, you can figure out the debit/credit direction for any account on an exam in under 3 seconds.

---

### The "Give and Receive" Mental Model

Every transaction is an exchange:

> _Something is received → Something is given in return._

The account that **receives** value → **Debit** (left — things coming in) The account that **gives** value → **Credit** (right — things going out)

```
Buy equipment with cash:
→ Equipment RECEIVES value → Debit Equipment
→ Cash GIVES value (leaves) → Credit Cash
```

This won't work perfectly in every edge case, but it's a strong intuition builder for most common transactions.

## 6. Common Mistakes — And How to Avoid Them

### Mistake 1 — Posting to the Wrong Side

**What happens:** Student debits the journal entry but posts it to the right (credit) side of the T-account by accident.

**Why it happens:** Rushing, or confusing journal entry format with T-account format.

**How to avoid it:** Always follow this checklist when posting:

```
□ Is this account DEBITED in the journal? → Post to LEFT of T-account
□ Is this account CREDITED in the journal? → Post to RIGHT of T-account
```

Never skip the check. One wrong side and your balance is off.

---

### Mistake 2 — Forgetting to Post Both Sides

**What happens:** Student posts the debit side but forgets to post the credit, or vice versa.

**Why it happens:** Each journal entry affects at least two accounts. It's easy to post one and move on.

**How to avoid it:** After posting an entry, tick off each line in the journal entry. If any line doesn't have a tick, it hasn't been posted yet. Only move to the next entry when every line is ticked.

---

### Mistake 3 — Adding Instead of Netting the Balance

**What happens:** A T-account has entries on both sides. Student adds them together instead of finding the difference.

**Example of the error:**

```
Cash T-account:
Left (Debit):  15,000 + 3,500 = 18,500
Right (Credit): 7,000 + 900  =  7,900

WRONG: Balance = 18,500 + 7,900 = 26,400  ✗
RIGHT: Balance = 18,500 − 7,900 = 10,600  ✓
```

**How to avoid it:** Always subtract. The balance is the **net difference**, always on the larger side.

---

### Mistake 4 — Confusing Normal Balances

**What happens:** Student sees a credit entry in the Cash account and thinks something is wrong, or calculates it as a credit balance.

**Why it happens:** They forget that even accounts with debit normal balances receive credits when they decrease.

**How to avoid it:** A credit entry in Cash just means cash went down. The **normal balance** is what the account looks like overall after netting both sides — not what individual entries look like.

---

### Mistake 5 — Treating Drawings as an Expense in T-Accounts

**What happens:** Student posts Drawings into an Expense T-account.

**Why it happens:** Both expenses and drawings are debited and both reduce equity — so they feel similar.

**How to avoid it:**

```
Expenses → Reduce equity through Net Income (appear on Income Statement)
Drawings → Reduce equity directly (appear on Equity Statement only)
```

They live in separate T-accounts and serve different purposes. Never mix them.
