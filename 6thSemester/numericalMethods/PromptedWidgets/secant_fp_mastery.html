Secant Method
& False Position (Regula Falsi)

Two methods. One geometric idea. Totally different philosophies. Master both and you can handle any root-finding problem your exam throws at you.

Secant Method
xi+1 = xi − f(xi)·(xi−xi-1) / (f(xi)−f(xi-1))
False Position
xr = (xU·f(xL) − xL·f(xU)) / (f(xL) − f(xU))
Section 01

Core Intuition

Both methods find where f(x) = 0. Both draw a secant line (straight line through two points on the curve) and use where it crosses the x-axis as the next guess. But they manage those two points in completely opposite ways.

💡 Secant — Mental Model

"Chase the x-axis with a sliding ruler." Draw a line through your two most recent points. Where it hits zero is your next guess. Then slide forward — drop the oldest point, keep the two newest. No safety net. Fast, bold, not always guaranteed.

💡 False Position — Mental Model

"Hunt with a safety net." Start with a bracket (one point above, one below the x-axis). Draw a line between them. Where it hits zero is your estimate. Replace one bracket endpoint — always keeping the root trapped. Slower. Always converges.

🔵 Secant Method — Geometric Picture

You have two guesses xi-1 and xi. They do not need to bracket the root — this is an open method. Draw the secant line through (xi-1, f(xi-1)) and (xi, f(xi)). Where it crosses y=0 is xi+1. Drop xi-1, use xi and xi+1 next time. The window always slides forward.

🔗 Direct Link to Newton-Raphson

Newton-Raphson: xi+1 = xi − f(xi)/f′(xi). The Secant method replaces the expensive exact derivative with a cheap approximation: f′(xi) ≈ [f(xi)−f(xi-1)] / (xi−xi-1). Same structure, no symbolic differentiation needed.

🟢 False Position — Geometric Picture

You choose xL and xU with f(xL) · f(xU) < 0 — opposite signs mean the root is between them. The secant line through these two points crosses y=0 at xr, which is smarter than the midpoint because it uses the function's shape. You then check which side still has the root and tighten the bracket. One endpoint always stays fixed — the one with the "dominant" sign.

💡 Why False Position Beats Bisection

Bisection always cuts at the midpoint, ignoring how the function behaves. False Position draws a line using f-values, so its estimate jumps closer to the root immediately — especially when the function is steep on one side and flat on the other. Your lecture says the secant x-intercept is "closer to the actual root compared to the midpoint."

⚡ The One-Sentence Difference

Secant

Open method. Slides forward using two most-recent points. Fast. Not guaranteed.

False Position

Bracketed method. Always traps the root. Guaranteed. Can be slow on asymmetric functions.

Section 02

Mathematical Derivation

🔵 Secant Method — Derived from Newton-Raphson

Step 1 — Start with Newton-Raphson
xi+1 = xi − f(xi) / f′(xi)

Problem: computing f′(xi) symbolically is expensive for computers.

Step 2 — Approximate the derivative (finite difference)
f′(xi) ≈ [f(xi) − f(xi-1)] / (xi − xi-1)

This is the slope of the secant line through the two points — the derivative definition without the limit.

Step 3 — Substitute into NR formula
xi+1 = xi − f(xi) ÷ [(f(xi)−f(xi-1)) / (xi−xi-1)]
Step 4 — Simplify (dividing = multiply by reciprocal)
xi+1 = xi − f(xi)·(xi−xi-1) / (f(xi)−f(xi-1)) ✓
📐 Also from Geometry (Similar Triangles — Your Lecture)

Triangles ABE ~ DCE in Figure 1. From proportionality: AB/AE = DC/DE, giving f(xi)/(xi−xi+1) = f(xi-1)/(xi-1−xi+1). Cross-multiply and solve for xi+1 → same formula. Two paths, one answer.

🟢 False Position — From Similar Triangles (Your Lecture)

From triangles ABC ~ DAE (your lecture's Figure): DE/AE = BC/AC, which gives:

// (0−f(x_L))/(x_r−x_L) = (0−f(x_U))/(x_r−x_U) Cross-multiply: (x_r − x_L)·f(x_U) = (x_r − x_U)·f(x_L) x_r·[f(x_L) − f(x_U)] = x_U·f(x_L) − x_L·f(x_U) x_r = [x_U·f(x_L) − x_L·f(x_U)] / [f(x_L) − f(x_U)] (Eq. 5) ✓ // Equivalently (Eq. 6 from your lecture): x_r = x_U − f(x_U)·(x_L − x_U) / (f(x_L) − f(x_U)) // Or (Eq. 7): x_r = x_L − f(x_L)·(x_U − x_L) / (f(x_U) − f(x_L)) // All three are mathematically identical. Your exam likely prefers Eq. 5.
💡 From Your Lecture

"Observe the resemblance of Equations (6) and (7) to the secant method." The formulas ARE the same structure — only the bracket update rule distinguishes the methods in practice.

Error Formula (identical for both)

|εₐ| = | (x_new − x_old) / x_new | × 100% // Secant: x_new = x_{i+1}, x_old = x_i // False Position: x_new = x_r^new, x_old = x_r^old (from previous iteration)
Section 03

Algorithms

🔵 Secant Algorithm

  1. Choose two guesses x₀ and x₁. Root need NOT be between them.
  2. Approximate derivative: f′ ≈ (f(x₁)−f(x₀))/(x₁−x₀)
  3. Compute x₂ = x₁ − f(x₁)·(x₁−x₀)/(f(x₁)−f(x₀))
  4. Compute error: |εₐ| = |x₂−x₁|/|x₂| × 100%
  5. If |εₐ| ≤ εₛ → stop. Else set x₀=x₁, x₁=x₂ → go to Step 3.
  6. Stop if max iterations exceeded.
⚠️ Guard

Check f(x₁) ≠ f(x₀) before dividing. Equal function values → division by zero → method fails.

🟢 False Position Algorithm

  1. Choose x_L, x_U such that f(x_L)·f(x_U) < 0. Root MUST be bracketed!
  2. Compute x_r = [x_U·f(x_L)−x_L·f(x_U)] / [f(x_L)−f(x_U)]
  3. Update bracket using f(x_L)·f(x_r):
    • < 0 → root in [x_L, x_r] → set x_U = x_r
    • > 0 → root in [x_r, x_U] → set x_L = x_r
    • = 0 → x_r is the exact root → stop!
  4. Compute error: |εₐ| = |x_r^new − x_r^old| / |x_r^new| × 100%
  5. If |εₐ| ≤ εₛ → stop. Else go to Step 2.

🧠 Mental Model + Quick Decision Guide

Choose Your Method in Under 10 Seconds

Newton-Raphson
"The surgeon — precise, fast, needs full information"
  • ✅ f(x) is easy to differentiate
  • ✅ You want maximum speed
  • ✅ Good initial guess available
  • ❌ Avoid if f′(x) is hard to compute
Secant Method
"The navigator — good speed, no map required"
  • ✅ f(x) is hard to differentiate
  • ✅ Two reasonable guesses available
  • ✅ Want NR-like speed without f′
  • ❌ Avoid if guarantee needed
False Position
"The safety climber — never falls, sometimes slow"
  • ✅ Need guaranteed convergence
  • ✅ You have a reliable bracket [a, b]
  • ✅ Accuracy > speed
  • ❌ Slow on highly asymmetric functions
3-Question Decision Tree
"Ask these in order"
  • Can I compute f′(x) easily? → Yes: use NR
  • Do I need guaranteed convergence? → Yes: use False Position
  • Otherwise → Secant Method
Section 06

Convergence & Pitfalls

🔵 Secant — Pitfalls

⚠️ Pitfall 1: Division by Zero

If f(xi) = f(xi-1), denominator = 0. The secant line is horizontal — never hits the x-axis. Always check this condition before dividing.

⚠️ Pitfall 2: Bad Initial Guesses (Mini Quiz from Lecture)

For sin(x) = 0, if both guesses are on the same "hill" of the sine curve (both positive), the secant line crosses the x-axis far from any intended root. The worst pair is one where f(x₀) ≈ f(x₁) (nearly same height) — tiny denominator, huge xi+1 jump. Always choose guesses with different-signed or very different-magnitude f-values when possible.

⚠️ Pitfall 3: Divergence on Oscillatory Functions

Just like NR, the method can diverge when the secant line sends the next guess far from the root — common with oscillating or highly nonlinear functions with no good initial guesses.

🟢 False Position — Pitfalls

⚠️ Pitfall: Stagnation (Stuck Bracket)

When the function is very asymmetric — one side extremely steep (large |f|), the other gentle (small |f|) — one bracket endpoint never gets replaced. The secant line always angles toward the gentle side. The bracket shrinks, but agonizingly slowly. This is the "same drawback as Bisection" your lecture mentions. Example: f(x) = x¹⁰ − 1 on [0, 1.5].

💡 Mini Quiz Answer — Slide 14: Bisection vs False Position

For the graph in your lecture (root near x_U, steep curve on right): False Position wins easily. The secant line immediately points to the root near x_U because f(x_U) is very small. Bisection wastes many iterations cutting at the midpoint far to the left. False Position's smarter estimate leaps directly toward the root in one or two iterations.

🚨 Common Student Mistakes

  • Secant: Using x-values where f-values belong — denominator must be f(xi) − f(xi-1), not xi − xi-1 alone.
  • Secant: Subtraction order error in denominator — it's f(xi) − f(xi-1) not the reverse.
  • False Position: Updating the wrong bracket endpoint — must check sign of f(x_L)·f(x_r), not f(x_r)·f(x_U).
  • False Position: Forgetting to verify f(x_L)·f(x_U) < 0 before starting.
  • Both: Error denominator is the NEW value, not the old one. Multiply by 100 for %.
  • Both: Not carrying enough decimal places through the iteration.
Section 07

Exam Strategy

⚡ Tip 1 — Write Formulas First

Before starting any calculation, write both formulas at the top. Annotate which part is numerator/denominator. This prevents formula confusion mid-calculation.

⚡ Tip 2 — Secant Table Setup

Columns: xi-1 | xi | f(xi-1) | f(xi) | xi+1 | |εₐ|. Each row shifts: old xi becomes new xi-1, old xi+1 becomes new xi.

⚡ Tip 3 — False Position Table

Columns: x_L | x_U | f(x_L) | f(x_U) | x_r | f(x_r) | Update | |εₐ|. Note which endpoint changes each row — one often stays constant.

⚡ Tip 4 — Bracket Check

After every FP iteration, multiply f(x_L)·f(x_r). Negative → root on left (x_U=x_r). Positive → root on right (x_L=x_r). Zero → found it. This is your single most important decision point.

⚡ Tip 5 — Derivation Question

If asked to derive Secant from NR: 3 steps. (1) Write NR formula. (2) Substitute finite-difference approximation for f′. (3) Simplify. Practice until it's automatic.

⚡ Tip 6 — Compare Question

Key comparison sentence: "Both methods use a secant line. Secant is open (no guaranteed convergence, superlinear speed). False Position is bracketed (guaranteed convergence, linear speed)."

Common Question Patterns

  1. "Apply Secant method to f(x) with x₀=a, x₁=b. Perform n iterations." → No derivative. Set up sliding table. Apply formula n times. Compute error after each.
  2. "Apply False Position to f(x) on [a,b]. Find root with tolerance ε." → Verify bracket first. Iterate with bracket update until |εₐ| ≤ ε.
  3. "Derive the Secant formula from Newton-Raphson." → Memorize 3-step derivation above.
  4. "Compare Secant and False Position." → Open vs bracketed. Convergence guarantee. Speed tradeoff. Failure modes.
  5. "Why is Secant preferred over Newton-Raphson when…" → Symbolic differentiation is expensive or impossible. Secant needs only function evaluations.
  6. "What is stagnation in False Position?" → One bracket endpoint freezes because function is asymmetric. Cite the large-|f| endpoint stays stuck.

Section 09

Deeper Understanding

🔵 Why Secant ≈ Newton-Raphson as Convergence Happens

The Secant method approximates f′(xi) ≈ Δf/Δx. As the method converges, xi → xi-1 → root, so Δx → 0 and the finite difference approaches the true derivative. This is why the Secant method's convergence rate (≈ 1.618, the golden ratio!) approaches but doesn't quite reach NR's quadratic rate (2.0). You're paying for not knowing the exact derivative with a slightly slower approach to the root.

🟢 Why the Bracket in False Position is Indestructible

By the Intermediate Value Theorem: if f is continuous and f(a) · f(b) < 0, there is at least one root in (a, b). Every False Position update is designed to preserve this sign condition. You only ever move an endpoint to xr when you know xr has the same sign as that endpoint — so the new sub-interval still has opposite signs. The root is always trapped. This is the mathematical guarantee behind "convergence is assured."

📜 The Ancient History (From Your Lecture)

Your lecture showed a slide of the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (~1650 BCE), an ancient Egyptian document. The original Regula Falsi ("rule of false position") was used to solve linear equations: guess a wrong answer, see how wrong you are, scale the guess. The modern bracketed False Position is a nonlinear generalization — same name, much more powerful idea. It is one of the oldest documented numerical algorithms in human history, over 3,500 years old.

✅ The Grand Unifying Idea

Newton-Raphson, Secant, and False Position all do the same thing: replace the curved function with a straight line and find where that line crosses zero. NR uses the tangent (1 point, needs derivative). Secant uses the secant through 2 recent points (no derivative). False Position uses the secant through 2 bracketing points (no derivative, adds bracket maintenance). Understanding this unifying idea means you truly understand all three methods — not just their formulas.
